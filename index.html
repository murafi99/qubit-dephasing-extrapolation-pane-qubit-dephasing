§4Bias-Variance Theory

Anchoring a regression to the exact relation f(T1) = 1/(2T1) trades a small, real bias for variance that scales with the anchored quantity's own magnitude — valid as a local expansion, not a global one, since an unrestricted Gaussian technically permits the reciprocal to blow up.

Proposition 1 — local, leading order in σ
Bias ≈ f(T1) · σ² (+0.64% at σ=0.08)
Var ≈ f(T1)² · σ²

Verified by 5,000,000-draw Monte Carlo: 0.652% observed vs. 0.640% predicted bias. The singularity at 1+ε=0 sits 12.5σ from the mean (P≈3.7×10⁻³⁶) — never encountered in any simulation run in this paper.

Curvature error ≠ fitted-estimator bias

An earlier draft implied Taylor's theorem gives the ridge estimator's bias directly. It doesn't — it only bounds the best tangent-line approximation. The actual fitted bias also depends on training distribution, regularization, and sample size.

§7Collinearity Diagnostic — the Critical Fix

Does decorrelating the two noisy training features change the outcome? The first analysis pooled 60 seed-condition points as if independent — but the same 15 seeds repeat across all 4 jitter levels, producing correlated (repeated-measures) data. Toggle below to see why that mattered.

Pooled OLS — treats 60 points as independent
Invalid — osprey slope +0.0609, 95% CI [−0.107, +0.228], p=0.470, not significant. Same-seed runs at different jitter levels are strongly correlated (seed 2000: ≈6.0% at J=0 vs. ≈6.8% at J=60) — this violates the independence assumption OLS standard errors rely on.
Corrected — fitting one slope per seed (15 independent estimates) and testing their mean: osprey slope +0.0609 (same point estimate), 95% CI [+0.011, +0.111], p=0.021, significant. Sherbrooke: p=0.011. The bug didn't change what the data said — it changed how confident we were allowed to be about it.
